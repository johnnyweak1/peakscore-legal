HRV by Age: Normal Ranges and Chart

November 23, 2026

HRV falls with age. In a study of 1,906 healthy adults, the average HRV (SDNN, the method Apple Watch uses) was around 50 ms at 25 to 34 and around 30 ms at 65 to 74. That is why one number for everyone makes no sense. Use the age tables below for orientation, then compare your HRV with your own baseline, which tells you much more.

Normal HRV by age and gender

The numbers come from Voss et al. (2015, PLOS One), one of the largest studies on HRV norms. Each cell shows the average, with the typical range for most healthy people in brackets.

There are two common ways to calculate HRV, SDNN and RMSSD, and they give different numbers for the same heartbeat. Check which one your device uses before you compare.

SDNN by age (the method Apple Watch uses)

Age Women Men
25-34 49 ms (30-68) 50 ms (29-71)
35-44 45 ms (25-66) 45 ms (28-61)
45-54 37 ms (23-51) 37 ms (22-51)
55-64 31 ms (18-43) 33 ms (18-48)
65-74 28 ms (16-40) 30 ms (16-43)

RMSSD by age (used by Oura, Whoop and many other wearables)

Age Women Men
25-34 43 ms (20-66) 40 ms (20-60)
35-44 35 ms (17-54) 32 ms (16-48)
45-54 26 ms (13-40) 23 ms (12-34)
55-64 21 ms (9-33) 20 ms (9-31)
65-74 19 ms (7-31) 19 ms (8-30)

These values were measured in a lab, over 5 minutes, lying down and awake. HRV is higher at night, so readings from your watch during sleep are often above these numbers. That is normal.

The study did not include people under 25. HRV keeps falling with age from early adulthood, so values in your late teens and early twenties are usually at or above the 25-34 group.

Apple Watch shows a different number than most HRV charts

Most HRV charts online use RMSSD. Apple Health stores HRV as SDNN. The two are calculated differently:

  • SDNN looks at how spread out all your heartbeat intervals are during a reading.
  • RMSSD looks at how much each interval differs from the one right before it.

The same heartbeats give two different values, so you cannot compare an Apple Watch reading with an RMSSD chart, or with a friend’s Oura or Whoop score. If you use Apple Watch, use the SDNN table above.

Why your sleeping HRV is the number to watch

During the day your HRV jumps around with every coffee, meeting, walk or workout. During sleep those influences are gone, and your heart rhythm reflects how recovered your body really is. HRV also follows a daily rhythm, so readings from different times of day are not comparable either (Natarajan et al., 2020, a study of 8 million wrist-worn trackers).

That is why the most useful HRV is the one taken while you sleep, compared night to night. It is also the HRV Peak Score uses: it reads last night’s sleeping HRV from Apple Health and scores it as one part of your daily Training Readiness.

Why HRV drops with age

HRV mostly reflects the parasympathetic nervous system, the “rest and digest” side that slows your heart and helps you recover. This system becomes less active with age, and it declines faster than the “fight or flight” side. The result is a lower HRV even in healthy, active people.

You cannot stop that trend, but you can slow it. Regular exercise, good sleep and less alcohol are linked to a higher HRV at any age, and in the Fitbit study more daily steps went together with better HRV.

Is your HRV good? Compare it with your baseline

Your HRV is personal. Two healthy people of the same age can be 30 ms apart, and both are normal. What matters is how your number moves compared with your usual level.

  1. Track your sleeping HRV for at least two to three weeks to see your normal range.
  2. Compare each morning’s number with that range, not with other people.
  3. Watch the direction over a week. A steady rise usually means you are adapting well. Several nights in a row clearly below your normal range suggest your body needs more recovery.

A single low night is rarely a concern. A short night, a late meal or a glass of wine is often enough to cause it.

What lowers your HRV at any age

  • Short or broken sleep: one of the strongest and fastest effects.
  • Hard training: HRV often dips for a day or two after an intense session, then returns to your normal level as you recover.
  • Alcohol: especially in the evening, it can lower sleeping HRV for the whole night.
  • Illness: HRV can drop even before you notice the first symptoms.
  • Stress: work, travel and emotional stress all show up in your HRV.

How to check your HRV on Apple Watch

Apple Watch records HRV automatically in the background, including during sleep. To see it, open the Health app on your iPhone, go to Browse, then Heart, then Heart Rate Variability.

The Health app shows the numbers, but it does not tell you what they mean for today’s workout. Peak Score does that for you: it takes your sleeping HRV together with your other recovery data and turns it into one clear call for the day: Rest, Keep Easy or Push Hard.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal HRV while sleeping?

HRV is usually higher during sleep than during the day, so a normal sleeping HRV is often above the daytime averages in the tables. The most useful comparison is your own sleeping HRV against your own average over the last few weeks.

Is Apple Watch HRV accurate?

Apple Watch measures HRV with an optical sensor, which works best when you are still. That is why readings taken during sleep are the most reliable. It is consistent enough to follow your own trend, but it is not a medical test.

Do men and women have different HRV?

Slightly. In large studies younger women and men have similar averages, and the gap disappears after about 55. Age and your own baseline matter far more than sex.

Why is my HRV lower than average for my age?

Genetics, fitness, sleep, stress, alcohol and recent hard training all move HRV. A number below the average is not a problem on its own. A clear drop below your own usual level is the signal worth paying attention to.

What HRV is too low?

There is no single cut-off that is too low for everyone. If your HRV is consistently far below your usual level and you also feel unwell, dizzy, short of breath or notice an irregular heartbeat, talk to a doctor.
